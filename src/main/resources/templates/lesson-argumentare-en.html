Lesson 1

Argumentation basics

A strong argument does not mean speaking loudly — it means connecting ideas clearly, supporting them with evidence, and anticipating counter-arguments.

1. Opinion vs. argument

Opinion: “I think online education is better.” — cannot be verified directly.

Argument: “Online education reduces costs by 40% and provides access to courses from top universities, which democratizes learning.” — has premise, mechanism, and conclusion.

Golden rule

Every sentence in an argument must answer “why?” or “how do we know that?”.

2. PRO / CONTRA structure

PRO

  • Define your position in 1 sentence
  • 2–3 main arguments
  • Evidence: studies, statistics, examples
  • Anticipate objection #1 and respond

CONTRA

  • Attack the PRO premise or evidence
  • Present counter-examples
  • Show ignored costs / risks
  • Conclude why the PRO position is incomplete

3. Logical fallacies to avoid

01
Ad hominem — attacking the person, not the idea.
02
Hasty generalization — “one case = all cases”.
03
False dilemma — “either A or B”, ignoring middle options.
04
Appeal to emotion — replacing evidence with fear or pity.

See the full catalog in Logical fallacies.

4. Pre-match checklist

  1. Do I clearly know what I am defending (PRO or CONTRA)?
  2. Do I have 2 arguments with concrete evidence?
  3. Do I have an anticipated counter-argument?
  4. Am I avoiding personal attacks and generalizations?
  5. Does my conclusion follow logically from the premise?
