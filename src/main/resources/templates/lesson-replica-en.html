Lesson 2

The art of rebuttal: respond, don't repeat

The most common mistake in debate: reintroducing the same argument as if your opponent said nothing. A good rebuttal attacks the premise or evidence — not the tone.

1. The PREL method (4 steps)

P — Pick up your opponent's idea in 1 sentence (“You say AI will take all jobs...”).

R — Reject a premise or generalization (“...but automation also creates new roles — IT history shows this”).

E — Exemplify with a concrete case (statistic, country, industry).

L — Link back to your position (“So the real risk is not total unemployment, but reskilling — and we can plan for that”).

Avoid

“That's not true” without saying what exactly is false and why.

2. Rebuttal types that score points

Premise rebuttal

“You assume all students have internet at home — 2024 data shows 12% do not.”

Mechanism rebuttal

“You say banning plastic reduces pollution — but 80% comes from industry, not individual consumption.”

Priority rebuttal

“Even if you are partly right, cost X outweighs benefit Y — so it is not the best solution now.”

3. Quick exercise (60 sec)

Read the argument: “Schools must ban phones because students no longer concentrate.” Write a PREL rebuttal without attacking students (“they are lazy”) — attack the link between concentration and banning.

Example: “You say lack of concentration comes from phones — but studies show short breaks can improve retention; the problem is use during explanations, not the phone's existence.”
